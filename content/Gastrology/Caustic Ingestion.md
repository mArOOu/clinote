> [!abstract] Caustic Ingestion
> Acute chemical injury to the gastrointestinal and upper respiratory tracts caused by the ingestion of strong alkalis ($\text{pH} > 11.5–12$) or strong acids ($\text{pH} < 2$). Spectrum ranges from superficial mucosal erythema to transmural necrosis, mediastinitis, peritonitis, and death.

> [!note] Pathophysiology
> - **Alkalis** cause *liquefactive necrosis*, saponifying fats and solubilizing proteins to penetrate deep tissue layers rapidly; predominantly injures the esophagus.
> - **Acids** cause *coagulative necrosis*, creating a protective surface eschar that limits deep penetration; predominantly injures the gastric antrum and pylorus.

> [!Tip]- Clinical PEARLS
> - The absence of oropharyngeal burns **does NOT** rule out severe downstream esophageal or gastric necrosis (occurs in up to 30% of significant ingestions).
> - Early diagnostic endoscopy (EGD) is optimal within **12–24 hours** (max 48 hours); **strictly avoid EGD between day 5 and day 14** due to maximum wall softening and highest risk of iatrogenic perforation.
> - The presence of **stridor, persistent drooling, and severe dysphagia** strongly predicts high-grade (Grade $\ge 2\text{b}$) gastrointestinal injury.
> - Never attempt chemical neutralization—the resulting exothermic reaction causes additional thermal tissue destruction.

> [!attention]- Clinical Presentation
> **History**
> - Ingestion of household or industrial cleaning agents, drain openers, battery acid, or button batteries (intentional vs. accidental).
> - Immediate oral/retrosternal burning pain, severe odynophagia, and inability to swallow secretions (drooling).
> - Epigastric pain, nausea, and hematemesis.
> - Hoarseness, dysphonia, or shortness of breath (airway involvement).
> 
> **Physical**
> - Stridor, tachypnea, hoarseness, and intercostal retractions (upper airway edema).
> - Continuous drooling and oropharyngeal erythema, ulcerations, or black/gray mucosal sloughing.
> - Subcutaneous emphysema of the neck or chest wall (esophageal perforation).
> - Abdominal rigidity, involuntary guarding, and rebound tenderness (gastric perforation / chemical peritonitis).

> [!check]- Diagnostic Criteria
> - **Mandatory Confirmation & Staging (Zargar Endoscopic Classification):**
>   - *Grade 0:* Normal mucosa.
>   - *Grade 1:* Mucosal edema and hyperemia.
>   - *Grade 2a:* Superficial ulcerations, erosions, exudates.
>   - *Grade 2b:* Deep discrete or circumferential ulcerations (high risk of stricture).
>   - *Grade 3a:* Scattered areas of necrosis (brown-black/gray discoloration).
>   - *Grade 3b:* Extensive, circumferential transmural necrosis (high risk of perforation/death).

> [!fail]- Further Investigations
> **Labs**
> - *Arterial/Venous Blood Gas + Lactate:* Severe metabolic acidosis and hyperlactatemia signal transmural bowel necrosis or impending shock.
> - *CBC, Coagulation Panel, BMP, Type and Screen:* Assess systemic leukocytosis ($>20,000/\mu\text{L}$ correlates with transmural injury), renal dysfunction, and prepare for emergency operative intervention.
> 
> **Imaging**
> - *Upright Chest & Abdominal Radiographs:* Initial screen for pneumoperitoneum (free air under diaphragm), pneumomediastinum, or pleural effusion.
> - *CT Neck/Chest/Abdomen with IV Contrast:* Superior first-line imaging to detect transmural necrosis, extraluminal fluid, mediastinitis, or occult perforation prior to or instead of EGD.
> - *Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD):* Definitive staging investigation within 12–24 hours in all symptomatic patients without peritonitis.
> 
> **Referrals**
> - *General / Cardiothoracic Surgery:* STAT notification for suspected perforation, peritonitis, or Grade 3b necrosis.
> - *ENT / Anesthesia:* STAT for emergent difficult airway control in patients with stridor or epiglottic burns.

> [!bug]- Red Flags
> - **Stridor / Airway Compromise:** Immediate threat of fatal laryngeal obstruction.
> - **Signs of Perforation:** Rigid "board-like" abdomen, subcutaneous crepitus, or Hamman's sign (mediastinal crunch).
> - **Refractory Shock / Severe Acidosis:** Indicates extensive multi-organ transmural chemical destruction.

> [!todo]- Differentials
> **Life-Threatening & Can't Miss**
> - Spontaneous Esophageal Rupture (Boerhaave Syndrome)
> - Thermal Epiglottitis / Inhalation Burn
> - Foreign Body / Button Battery Impaction (esophago-aortic fistula risk)
> 
> **Most Likely**
> - Reflux Esophagitis / Infectious Esophagitis
> - Mallory-Weiss Tear
> 
> **Most Surprising**
> - Intentional Multi-Toxin Ingestion masquerading as isolated caustic burn
> 
> **Less Common**
> - Acute Paraquat Poisoning (corrosive oral injury + pulmonary fibrosis)
> 
> **Non-Critical**
> - Accidental low-concentration taste ingestion (e.g., household vinegar, mild detergent)

> [!summary]- Management
> **Treatment Goals**
> - **Primary Target:** Secure definitive airway, maintain organ perfusion, and rapidly identify/excise perforated or non-viable transmural necrotic tissue.
> - **Secondary Target:** Prevent esophageal stricture formation and bacterial superinfection.
> 
> **Immediate / Emergencies**
> - **Perform Awake Fiberoptic Intubation or Emergency Surgical Airway (Cricothyroidotomy)** immediately upon the presence of stridor, respiratory distress, or severe laryngeal edema to secure a definitive airway before complete swelling occurs.
> - **Maintain strict NPO status and initiate IV Isotonic Crystalloid Resuscitation** STAT upon presentation to correct hypovolemia and support visceral perfusion.
> 
> **Definitive Staging & Targeted Therapy**
> - **Perform CT Neck/Chest/Abdomen with IV contrast** within the first 6 hours to grade wall necrosis and rule out transmural perforation.
> - **Perform Early Diagnostic EGD** within 12–24 hours of ingestion in stable patients lacking clinical/radiographic signs of perforation to stage mucosal injury (Zargar score).
> - **Administer IV Proton Pump Inhibitor (Pantoprazole 80 mg IV bolus, then 8 mg/h infusion or 40 mg BID)** immediately to reduce acid-mediated secondary mucosal damage.
> - **Administer IV Broad-Spectrum Antibiotics (e.g., Piperacillin-Tazobactam or Ceftriaxone + Metronidazole)** ONLY if transmural necrosis (Grade 3), suspected perforation, pulmonary aspiration, or systemic sepsis is identified.
> 
> **Surgical Escalation**
> - **Perform Emergent Exploratory Laparotomy $\pm$ Thoracotomy with Esophagectomy / Gastrectomy** STAT for patients with visible perforation, persistent peritonitis, or Grade 3b transmural necrosis on CT/EGD to eliminate fatal source infection.
> 
> **Disposition & Surveillance**
> - **Admit directly to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU)** for all patients with Grade $\ge 2\text{b}$ burns, airway compromise, or intentional high-volume ingestions.
> - **Discharge safely from the ED** after 6 hours of observation ONLY in asymptomatic patients with accidental low-concentration ingestion who tolerate oral intake and show normal baseline imaging.

> [!warning]- Contraindications & Pitfalls
> - **Avoid inducing emesis (e.g., Ipecac)** in caustic ingestion to prevent re-exposing the esophagus and pharynx to the corrosive agent and triggering aspiration.
> - **Avoid chemical neutralizing agents (weak acids or weak bases)** to prevent generating an exothermic reaction that produces severe secondary thermal burns.
> - **Avoid blind insertion of nasogastric (NG) tubes or gastric lavage** to prevent mechanical perforation of the friable, necrotic esophageal wall.
> - **Avoid administering Activated Charcoal** to prevent lack of binding efficacy, obscuring the endoscopic mucosal view, and precipitating lethal pneumonitis if aspirated.
> - **Avoid routine systemic corticosteroids** in high-grade burns to prevent increasing the risk of occult perforation and systemic infection without reducing stricture rates.

> [!error]- Complications
> - Esophageal / Gastric Perforation $\rightarrow$ subcutaneous emphysema, rigid abdomen, mediastinitis $\rightarrow$ emergency broad-spectrum antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, and emergent surgical exploration.
> - Esophageal Stricture Formation (seen in $>70\%$ of Grade 2b/3 burns) $\rightarrow$ progressive dysphagia developing 3–8 weeks post-ingestion $\rightarrow$ endoscopic balloon dilatation or surgical reconstruction.
> - Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma $\rightarrow$ malignant transformation occurring 20–40 years post-injury (1000-fold increased risk) $\rightarrow$ lifelong endoscopic surveillance starting 15–20 years post-ingestion.

> [!success]- Typical Scenario
> A 34-year-old male is brought to the ED 2 hours after an intentional ingestion of an industrial lye drain cleaner (alkali). He presents with severe retrosternal pain, continuous drooling, a raspy voice, and mild inspiratory stridor. Fiberoptic nasal endoscopy reveals extensive supraglottic edema, prompting immediate awake intubation. Urgent IV contrast CT of the chest and abdomen rules out free mediastinal or peritoneal air, and EGD performed at 14 hours reveals circumferential Grade 3a necrosis of the mid-esophagus. He is transferred to the ICU on IV PPI therapy and broad-spectrum antibiotics with close surgical monitoring.

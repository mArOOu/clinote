> [!abstract] Acute Lower GI Bleeding
> Acute intraluminal hemorrhage originating distal to the ligament of Treitz (primarily colon, rectum, or anus), classically presenting as hematochezia; ~80–85% resolve spontaneously without intervention.

> [!note] Pathophysiology
>
> - **Diverticular bleed (most common):** Asymmetric intimal thickening and medial thinning of the **vasa recta** as it drapes over the dome of a colonic diverticulum $\rightarrow$ painless arterial rupture.
> - **Ischemic colitis:** Transient colonic hypoperfusion in "watershed" areas (splenic flexure/Griffith point, rectosigmoid junction/Sudeck point) $\rightarrow$ mucosal sloughing and bloody diarrhea.
> - **Angiodysplasia:** Degenerative dilation of submucosal veins and capillaries (predominantly right colon) $\rightarrow$ low-grade venous oozing or brisk bleeding (associated with aortic stenosis = **Heyde syndrome** via acquired vWF deficiency).

> [!Tip]- Clinical PEARLS
>
> - **Rule out UGIB first:** Up to 15% of patients with severe hematochezia and hemodynamic instability have a brisk **upper** GI bleed; perform an EGD if CTA is negative or if BUN:Cr >30:1.
> - **Diverticulosis vs. Diverticulitis:** Diverticular bleeding is **painless** arterial bleeding; diverticulitis causes LLQ pain/fever and _rarely_ presents with significant bleeding.
> - **CTA before Colonoscopy in unstable patients:** If Shock Index (HR/SBP) >1 or brisk ongoing bleeding, go straight to **CT Angiography (CTA)** $\rightarrow$ IR embolization (unprepped emergency colonoscopy has poor visualization and low yield).
> - **Oakland Score ≤8:** Validated threshold to safely discharge stable patients from the ED for outpatient investigation.

> [!attention]- Clinical Presentation
> **History**
>
> - **Hematochezia:** Maroon stools or bright red blood per rectum (BRBPR).
> - **Painless, large-volume bleeding:** Classic for diverticular hemorrhage or angiodysplasia.
> - **Cramping abdominal pain followed by bloody diarrhea:** Classic for ischemic colitis or infectious/inflammatory colitis.
> - **Outlet-type bleeding:** Bright red blood dripping into bowl or on toilet paper, often with painful defecation (fissure) or painless prolapse (internal hemorrhoids).
> - **Medication history:** NSAIDs, antiplatelets, anticoagulants, recent post-polypectomy (up to 14–30 days prior).
>
> **Physical**
>
> - **Hemodynamic assessment:** Calculate **Shock Index (HR / SBP)**; SI >1 indicates hemodynamic instability and active bleeding.
> - **Abdominal exam:** Soft/non-tender in diverticular/angiodysplasia bleed; tenderness out of proportion or mild LLQ pain in ischemic colitis; peritonitis suggests perforation/transmural ischemia.
> - **Digital Rectal Exam (DRE) & Anoscopy:** Mandatory to confirm stool color, exclude palpable rectal masses, and identify hemorrhoids or fissures.

> [!check]- Diagnostic Criteria
>
> - **Colonoscopy:** Direct visualization of an active bleeding source or stigmata of recent hemorrhage (active oozing/spurting, non-bleeding visible vessel, or adherent clot) in the colon or rectum.
> - **CT Angiography (CTA):** Active intraluminal extravasation of IV contrast within the lower GI tract (requires active bleeding rate ≥0.3–0.5 mL/min).
> - **Oakland Score ≤8:** Absence of high-risk features (incorporating age, sex, prior LGIB, DRE findings, HR, SBP, and Hb) confirming minor bleed safe for immediate outpatient management.

> [!fail]- Further Investigations
> **Labs**
>
> - **Initial:** Type and crossmatch, CBC, CMP, PT/INR, aPTT, lactate (elevated in ischemic colitis or shock).
> - **Interpretation:** **BUN:Cr ratio <20:1** supports LGIB (if >30:1, strongly suspect brisk UGIB).
>
> **Imaging**
>
> - **CT Angiography (CTA):** **First-line diagnostic test** in hemodynamically unstable patients (Shock Index >1) or brisk ongoing bleeding to localize the site for IR embolization.
> - **Tagged RBC Scintigraphy:** **NOT routinely recommended** in acute management (slow, imprecise localization compared to CTA).
>
> **Referrals**
>
> - **Gastroenterology:** Inpatient colonoscopy (after full bowel prep) for stable admitted patients (Oakland >8).
> - **Interventional Radiology (IR):** Immediate consultation if CTA shows active contrast extravasation for superselective transcatheter arterial embolization.
> - **Colorectal / General Surgery:** Salvage consultation if endoscopic and IR therapies fail, or if transmural bowel ischemia/perforation is suspected.

> [!bug]- Red Flags
>
> - Hemodynamic instability or **Shock Index (HR/SBP) >1**
> - Ongoing brisk hematochezia or syncope
> - **Oakland Score >8**
> - Peritoneal signs or elevated lactate (suspect transmural ischemic colitis)
> - Active anticoagulation or coagulopathy

> [!todo]- Differentials
> **Life-Threatening & Can't Miss**
>
> - **Brisk Upper GI Bleed:** Massive duodenal ulcer or variceal bleed with rapid transit (~15% of severe hematochezia).
> - **Diverticular Bleeding:** Most common cause of major LGIB in adults (30–50%); painless, arterial, self-limiting in ~80%.
> - **Ischemic Colitis:** Older patients with vascular risk factors or recent hypotension; abdominal pain + bloody diarrhea.
> - **Colorectal Adenocarcinoma:** Chronic occult bleeding or acute hematochezia; altered bowel habits, weight loss, iron-deficiency anemia.
>
> **Most Likely**
>
> - **Anorectal Disease (Hemorrhoids, Anal Fissure):** Most common overall cause in patients <50 years; small volume, bright red blood coating stool.
> - **Angiodysplasia (Vascular Ectasia):** Common in elderly, CKD, or aortic stenosis; right-sided preponderance.
> - **Post-Polypectomy Bleeding:** History of colonoscopy with polypectomy within the past 2–4 weeks.
>
> **Most Surprising**
>
> - **Meckel Diverticulum:** Painless massive brick-red hematochezia in children/young adults due to ectopic gastric mucosa ulcerating adjacent ileal tissue.
>
> **Less Common**
>
> - **Inflammatory Bowel Disease (UC > Crohn's):** Bloody diarrhea with urgency, tenesmus, and systemic symptoms.
> - **Radiation Proctopathy:** History of pelvic radiation (e.g., prostate or cervical cancer) causing mucosal telangiectasias.
>
> **Non-Critical**
>
> - **Infectious Colitis:** _Campylobacter_, _Salmonella_, _Shigella_, EHEC, _C. difficile_.

> [!summary]- Management
> **Treatment Goals**
>
> - **Primary Target:** Hemodynamic stability (MAP ≥65 mmHg, Shock Index <1), **Hb 7–9 g/dL** (or **≥8 g/dL** in active CAD/ACS), definitive hemostasis.
> - **Secondary Target:** Prevent rebleeding, avoid blind surgical resection, and balance thromboembolic vs. bleeding risks when resuming antithrombotics.
>
> **Immediate / Emergencies**
>
> - **Place two large-bore IVs (≥18G) and infuse isotonic crystalloids** upon presentation with hemodynamic instability or Shock Index >1 to restore intravascular volume.
> - **Transfuse pRBCs** using a restrictive threshold of **Hb <7 g/dL** (or **<8 g/dL** in patients with cardiovascular disease) to maintain tissue oxygenation without worsening bleeding.
> - **Reverse anticoagulation immediately** using **4-factor PCC + IV Vitamin K** (for warfarin) or **Andexanet alfa / Idarucizumab** (for DOACs) ONLY in patients with life-threatening hemorrhage and persistent hemodynamic instability.
>
> **Diagnostic & Therapeutic Localization (Unstable vs. Stable Pathway)**
>
> - **Perform emergent CT Angiography (CTA)** as the initial diagnostic step in hemodynamically unstable patients (**Shock Index >1**) or those with brisk ongoing bleeding to rapidly localize the bleeding vessel.
> - **Proceed to Transcatheter Arterial Embolization (TAE)** via IR within 60–90 minutes if CTA demonstrates active contrast extravasation to achieve targeted arterial occlusion.
> - **Perform EGD** immediately if CTA is negative in a hemodynamically unstable patient with hematochezia to rule out a brisk upper GI source.
> - **Administer 4–6 L of Polyethylene Glycol (PEG) bowel preparation** once hemodynamically stabilized prior to inpatient colonoscopy to clear intraluminal clots and maximize diagnostic/therapeutic yield.
> - **Perform inpatient colonoscopy** following complete bowel prep in stable high-risk patients (**Oakland Score >8**) to apply endoscopic hemostasis (through-the-scope clips for diverticular/post-polypectomy bleeds; **argon plasma coagulation [APC]** for angiodysplasia/radiation proctopathy).
>
> **Stepwise Escalation & Antithrombotic Management**
>
> - **Perform surgical segmental colectomy** ONLY as a salvage measure when both endoscopic and angiographic interventions fail and the bleeding site has been definitively localized.
> - **Continue aspirin for secondary cardiovascular prevention** uninterrupted during LGIB (or resume within 24–48 hours of hemostasis) to prevent fatal major adverse cardiac events.
> - **Hold P2Y12 inhibitors (clopidogrel, ticagrelor) and DOACs/warfarin** during active bleeding, and **resume within 5–7 days** once hemostasis is achieved (after cardiology consult if recent coronary stent <3–6 months).
>
> **Disposition & Follow-up**
>
> - **Discharge home from the ED** for outpatient colonoscopy if the patient is hemodynamically stable with an **Oakland Score ≤8** and no other indications for admission.
> - **Admit to a monitored bed or ICU** if **Oakland Score >8**, Shock Index >1, or severe comorbidities to allow resuscitation, CTA/colonoscopy, and serial Hb monitoring.
> - **Discontinue NSAIDs permanently** in all patients with diverticular bleeding to significantly reduce long-term recurrence risk.

> [!warning]- Contraindications & Pitfalls
>
> - **Avoid unprepped colonoscopy** in acute LGIB to prevent cecal intubation failure, missed lesions, and iatrogenic perforation from poor visualization.
> - **Avoid blind subtotal colectomy** (surgery without prior preoperative localization via CTA or colonoscopy) to prevent massive morbidity, high mortality (up to 30%), and persistent rebleeding.
> - **Avoid thermal contact coagulation monotherapy in the right colon or dome of a diverticulum** due to the thin colonic wall/absence of muscularis propria to prevent colonic perforation (prefer mechanical clips or band ligation for diverticula).
> - **Avoid Tranexamic Acid (TXA)** in acute GI bleeding (per HALT-IT trial) as it does not reduce mortality and increases venous thromboembolism and seizure risk.
> - **Avoid barium enema** in acute LGIB as it obscures visualization for subsequent colonoscopy and CTA/angiography.

> [!error]- Complications
>
> - **Bowel Infarction / Perforation:** Complication of superselective IR embolization (~1–4% risk) or severe ischemic colitis $\rightarrow$ monitor for worsening abdominal pain, fever, peritonitis, and rising lactate; requires surgical resection.
> - **Contrast-Induced Acute Kidney Injury (CI-AKI):** Risk following CTA + IR angiography $\rightarrow$ ensure aggressive IV crystalloid resuscitation before/after contrast (do not withhold life-saving CTA solely for elevated Cr).
> - **Thromboembolic Events (MI, Stroke, Stent Thrombosis):** Caused by prolonged cessation of antiplatelets/anticoagulants $\rightarrow$ resume aspirin immediately and anticoagulants within 7 days.

> [!success]- Typical Scenario
> A 74-year-old woman with hypertension and constipation presents with sudden, painless passage of large-volume maroon stools and lightheadedness. Vitals show HR 118 bpm and BP 98/62 mmHg (Shock Index = 1.2); Hb is 7.6 g/dL. After rapid resuscitation with two 18G IVs and isotonic crystalloids, an emergent **CT Angiography (CTA)** is performed (bypassing immediate colonoscopy due to Shock Index >1), revealing active contrast extravasation in the sigmoid colon from a diverticular bleed, which is successfully treated via **IR superselective coil embolization**.

> [!abstract] Acute Spinal Cord Compression & Cauda Equina Syndrome
> Time-critical neurological emergencies resulting from mechanical compression of the spinal cord (above L1–L2, producing an upper motor neuron syndrome) or the lumbosacral nerve roots of the cauda equina (below L1–L2, producing a lower motor neuron syndrome). Rapid diagnosis and surgical decompression within $< 24–48\text{ hours}$ are essential to prevent permanent paraplegia, fecal/urinary incontinence, and sexual dysfunction.

> [!note] Pathophysiology
> - **Anatomical Boundary (Conus Medullaris vs. Cauda Equina):**
>   - The spinal cord terminates as the conus medullaris at the **L1–L2 vertebral level** in adults.
>   - Compression above L1–L2 involves the cord directly $\rightarrow$ **Myelopathy** with spasticity, hyperreflexia, extensor plantar responses (Babinski), and a distinct sensory level.
>   - Compression below L1–L2 compresses the intradural lumbosacral nerve roots (L2–S5) $\rightarrow$ **Cauda Equina Syndrome** with flaccid paresis, areflexia, and lower motor neuron signs.
> - **Etiologies:**
>   - Massive central lumbar disc herniation (most common non-neoplastic cause, especially at L4–L5 and L5–S1).
>   - Metastatic epidural spinal cord compression (MESCC: breast, lung, prostate, renal, myeloma).
>   - Spinal epidural hematoma (coagulopathy, post-epidural puncture).
>   - Spinal epidural abscess (Staph aureus, IV drug use, bacteremia, diabetes).

> [!tip]- Clinical PEARLS
> - **CES with Incomplete Retention (CESI) vs. CES with Retention (CESR):**
>   - *CESI (Urgent surgical priority):* Patient has urinary altered sensation, straining, or hesitancy, but is *not yet* in complete retention. Decompression produces up to 90% full functional recovery.
>   - *CESR (Advanced):* Painless urinary overflow incontinence and established neurogenic retention. Poorer functional prognosis, but emergent surgery is still indicated.
> - **Post-Void Residual (PVR) is the single most sensitive screening test:** A post-void bladder scan volume $> 100–200\text{ mL}$ in a patient with acute bilateral sciatica/back pain has $> 90\%$ sensitivity for cauda equina syndrome.
> - **Saddle Anesthesia Testing:** Systematically test light touch and pinprick sensation in dermatomes S3–S5 (perianal, perineal, and medial buttocks).
> - **The Classic Triad of Spinal Epidural Abscess:** Fever + localized back pain + progressive neurological deficit (complete triad present in only $< 20\%$ at early presentation).

> [!attention]- Clinical Presentation
> **Cauda Equina Syndrome (Lumbosacral Root Compression)**
> - Bilateral sciatica / radicular pain extending down both legs.
> - **Saddle anesthesia** (loss of sensation over buttocks, perineum, perianal region).
> - **Bladder dysfunction:** Urinary hesitancy, diminished urinary stream, poor stream sensation, progress to overflow incontinence and painless urinary retention.
> - **Bowel dysfunction:** Fecal incontinence, loss of anal sphincter tone, inability to feel stool passing.
> - Sexual dysfunction (impotence, loss of penile sensation, loss of clitoral sensation).
> - Lower extremity flaccid motor weakness (foot drop, ankle weakness) and absent Achilles reflexes.
> 
> **Acute Spinal Cord Compression (Thoracic / Cervical Myelopathy)**
> - Progressive bilateral leg weakness, stiff spastic gait, ascending paresthesias.
> - Sensory level on the torso (e.g., T4 nipples, T10 umbilicus).
> - Hyperreflexia, sustained ankle clonus, bilateral positive Babinski signs.

> [!check]- Diagnostic Criteria
> - **Clinical Cauda Equina Criteria:** Bilateral lumbosacral radiculopathy + objective saddle sensory disturbance + confirmed neurogenic bladder/bowel dysfunction (elevated PVR $> 200\text{ mL}$ or sphincter atony).
> - **MRI Confirmation:** Demonstration of severe canal stenosis with complete obliteration of the thecal sac and compression of the cauda equina roots or spinal cord.

> [!fail]- Further Investigations
> - **Urgent Whole-Spine MRI (Gold Standard):** Must image the entire spine (not just lumbar) because thoracic cord compression can mimic cauda equina signs, and 20% of spine metastases are non-contiguous.
> - **CT Myelography:** Indicated only if MRI is strictly contraindicated (pacemaker, non-MRI compatible metallic implants).
> - **Bladder Ultrasound (PVR):** Immediate bedside test to objectively quantify post-void residual volume.
> - **Infectious Workup (if suspecting epidural abscess):** Blood cultures (x2 sets), ESR, CRP, CBC.

> [!bug]- Red Flags
> - New-onset urinary retention or overflow incontinence in the context of back pain.
> - Progressive numbness in the perianal / saddle region.
> - Sudden bilateral lower extremity weakness or foot drop.
> - Severe localized unremitting back pain with fever in an immunocompromised host or IV drug user.

> [!todo]- Differentials
> - Guillain-Barré Syndrome (ascending flaccid paralysis with areflexia, but spared bladder early and albuminocytologic dissociation).
> - Acute Transverse Myelitis.
> - Severe diabetic amyotrophy or bilateral lumbosacral plexopathy.
> - Spinal Cord Infarction (anterior spinal artery syndrome).

> [!summary]- Management
> **1. Immediate Medical Measures**
> - Foley catheterization to relieve bladder distension and avoid detrusor rupture; record initial catheterized volume.
> - **Corticosteroids:** If tumoral/metastatic cord compression is suspected, administer **Dexamethasone 10–16 mg IV bolus** immediately, followed by 4–8 mg IV q6h with PPI gastroprotection (rapidly reduces vasogenic cord edema).
> - **Empiric Antibiotics (if Epidural Abscess suspected):** Ceftriaxone 2g IV q12h + Vancomycin 15–20 mg/kg IV q12h; obtain blood cultures prior to initiation if patient is neurologically stable.
> 
> **2. Emergent Neurosurgical Decompression**
> - **Timing:** Decompression within **$< 24–48\text{ hours}$** from symptom onset is associated with dramatically better bladder and motor recovery (especially in CESI).
> - **Surgical Procedure:** Urgent posterior lumbar laminectomy, microdiscectomy, or bilateral decompression to widely unroof the cauda equina nerve roots. For epidural abscess, open laminectomy and pus evacuation with thorough washout.

> [!warning]- Contraindications & Pitfalls
> - **Do NOT delay MRI for daytime hours:** Cauda equina syndrome is a surgical emergency; delay leads to irreversible sphincter paralysis.
> - **Do not rely on anal tone alone:** Digital rectal exam has variable sensitivity; subjective saddle sensory loss and post-void residual are more reliable early.
> - **Do not forget to image the whole spine** in cancer patients.

> [!error]- Complications
> - Permanent neurogenic bladder requiring lifelong clean intermittent self-catheterization (CIC).
> - Permanent fecal incontinence with loss of anal sphincter function.
> - Permanent sexual dysfunction.
> - Paraplegia and chronic neuropathic pain.

> [!success]- Typical Scenario
> A 42-year-old woman with a history of recurrent L5 sciatica presents with worsening bilateral leg pain and numbness over the past 36 hours. She reports that she has not urinated in 10 hours and "does not feel the urge to go." On examination, pinprick sensation is severely blunted in the perineal and perianal region (S3–S5). Deep tendon reflexes at the ankles are absent bilaterally. Bedside ultrasound reveals 850 mL of retained urine in the bladder; a catheter is inserted immediately. An emergency whole-spine MRI performed within 2 hours demonstrates a massive extruded L4–L5 disc fragment occupying 90% of the spinal canal with complete compression of the cauda equina. The on-call spine surgeon takes her immediately to the operating room for an emergency L4–L5 decompressive laminectomy and fragmentectomy. At 6-week follow-up, her saddle sensation has normalized and she has achieved spontaneous bladder emptying with normal post-void residual volumes.

> [!abstract] Spinal Trauma
> Traumatic mechanical disruption of the osseous, ligamentous, and disco-vertebral column $\pm$ injury to the spinal cord or nerve roots. Manifests as stable or unstable fractures, dislocations, and Traumatic Spinal Cord Injury (SCI). The initial clinical priority is strict full-spine immobilization and aggressive avoidance of hypotension to preserve spinal cord perfusion pressure.

> [!note] Pathophysiology
> - **Three-Column Concept of Denis:**
>   - **Anterior Column:** Anterior longitudinal ligament (ALL) + anterior 2/3 of vertebral body and annulus.
>   - **Middle Column (Crucial for stability):** Posterior 1/3 of vertebral body and annulus + posterior longitudinal ligament (PLL).
>   - **Posterior Column:** Pedicles, facets, laminae, spinous processes, interspinous and supraspinous ligaments, ligamentum flavum. Disruption of $\ge 2$ columns indicates spinal instability.
> - **Primary vs. Secondary Spinal Cord Injury:**
>   - *Primary Injury:* Immediate contusion, transection, or compression of neural parenchyma and microvasculature at the moment of trauma.
>   - *Secondary Injury:* Progressive post-traumatic microvascular ischemia, loss of autoregulation, lipid peroxidation, cytotoxic edema, and free radical-induced apoptosis.
> - **Neurogenic Shock vs. Spinal Shock:**
>   - *Neurogenic Shock:* Hemodynamic distributive collapse caused by loss of sympathetic vasomotor tone from cervical or high thoracic ($> \text{T6}$) cord injury $\rightarrow$ profound hypotension + paradoxical **bradycardia** + warm, dry extremities.
>   - *Spinal Shock:* Physiological, transient complete loss of all spinal cord reflexes, motor power, and sensory function below the lesion level (bulbocavernosus reflex absent). Resolution is marked by the return of the bulbocavernosus reflex and subsequent spasticity.

> [!tip]- Clinical PEARLS
> - **The MAP 85–90 mmHg Rule:** Maintain mean arterial pressure (MAP) between **85 and 90 mmHg for 7 days** using volume resuscitation and vasopressors (Norepinephrine or Epinephrine) to optimize capillary perfusion to the injured spinal cord (AANS/CNS Spine Trauma Guidelines).
> - **Neurogenic Shock: Hypotension WITH Bradycardia:** Unlike hemorrhagic shock (hypotension + tachycardia), neurogenic shock presents with bradycardia due to unopposed parasympathetic vagal tone.
> - **NEXUS Criteria for C-Spine Clearance (All 5 must be met to clear clinically without imaging):**
>   1. No posterior midline cervical spine tenderness.
>   2. No evidence of intoxication.
>   3. Normal alertness (GCS 15).
>   4. No focal neurological deficit.
>   5. No painful distracting injuries.
> - **Priapism is a sign of complete high-cord transection:** Sustained erection in an unresponsive trauma patient signals profound loss of supraspinal sympathetic inhibitory tone.

> [!attention]- Clinical Presentation
> **Incomplete Spinal Cord Syndromes**
> - **Central Cord Syndrome (Most common):** Hyperextension injury in older adults with cervical spondylosis. Motor weakness **upper extremities > lower extremities** with variable sensory loss (cervical fibers sit centrally in corticospinal tract).
> - **Anterior Cord Syndrome:** Flexion injury or anterior spinal artery occlusion. Complete loss of motor function, pain, and temperature sensation below lesion; **spares vibration and proprioception** (dorsal columns intact). Worst prognosis for functional recovery.
> - **Brown-Séquard Syndrome:** Hemisection of the cord (penetrating trauma or unilateral facet fracture). **Ipsilateral** motor loss and proprioception/vibration loss; **contralateral** loss of pain and temperature 1–2 levels below lesion.
> 
> **Physical Examination**
> - Log-roll examination: Palpate entire midline spine for tenderness, step-offs, and hematomas.
> - Standardized ASIA Impairment Scale examination (Sensory levels C2–S4/5, Motor key muscle groups 0–5).
> - Digital rectal examination: Assess voluntary anal sphincter contraction (defines complete vs. incomplete injury) and deep anal pressure. Test **bulbocavernosus reflex** (pulling Foley catheter or squeezing glans while palpating anal sphincter).

> [!check]- Diagnostic Criteria
> - **ASIA Impairment Scale (AIS):**
>   - **A (Complete):** No motor or sensory function preserved in the sacral segments S4–S5.
>   - **B (Sensory Incomplete):** Sensory but not motor function preserved below neurological level, including S4–S5.
>   - **C (Motor Incomplete):** Motor function preserved below neurological level, and more than half of key muscle functions have a muscle grade $< 3$.
>   - **D (Motor Incomplete):** Motor function preserved below neurological level, and at least half of key muscle functions have a muscle grade $\ge 3$.
>   - **E (Normal):** Motor and sensory scores are normal.
> - **Thoracolumbar Injury Classification and Severity Score (TLICS):** Based on injury morphology (compression, burst, translational, distraction), posterior ligamentous complex (PLC) integrity, and neurological status. Score $> 4$ indicates operative management.

> [!fail]- Further Investigations
> - **Multidetector CT of Spine (Whole Spine if high energy):** Fast, highly sensitive gold standard for evaluating bony fractures, facet subluxations, canal compromise, and pedicle/lamina fractures.
> - **Spinal MRI (T1, T2, STIR, Diffusion):** Gold standard for visualizing cord contusion, cord edema, intramedullary hemorrhage (worse prognosis), epidural hematoma, ligamentous rupture, and disc herniation causing neural compression.
> - **CT Angiogram (CTA) Neck:** Indicated in cervical spine fractures involving the transverse foramen or facet subluxation to rule out **Vertebral Artery Dissection / Occlusion**.

> [!bug]- Red Flags
> - Ascending motor deficit or respiratory compromise (injury approaching C3–C5 "keeps the diaphragm alive").
> - Hemodynamic collapse with bradycardia (neurogenic shock requiring inotropic vasopressor support).
> - Complete loss of sensation in sacral segments S4–S5 (loss of sacral sparing).
> - Absent voluntary anal contraction and absent deep anal sensation.

> [!todo]- Differentials
> - Acute Disc Herniation without fracture.
> - Spinal Epidural Abscess or Epidural Hematoma.
> - Guillain-Barré Syndrome or Transverse Myelitis (non-traumatic acute paralysis).
> - Cauda Equina Syndrome (fracture below L1–L2 affecting lumbar/sacral roots).

> [!summary]- Management
> **1. Emergent Pre-Hospital & ED Stabilization**
> - Strict spinal precautions: Cervical rigid collar, vacuum mattress, log-roll protocol for all transfers.
> - Airway: Early intubation with manual in-line stabilization if C3–C5 level injury or vital capacity $< 15\text{ mL/kg}$.
> - Hemodynamic Optimization: Resuscitate aggressively. Maintain **$\text{MAP} \ge 85–90\text{ mmHg}$ for 7 days** using IV fluids and Norepinephrine ($\pm$ Phenylephrine / Epinephrine).
> - Foley catheter insertion: Prevent acute bladder distension and monitor hourly urine output.
> 
> **2. Medical & Pharmacological Management**
> - **High-dose Methylprednisolone (NASCIS protocol):** No longer recommended as standard of care by AANS/CNS guidelines due to lack of definitive clinical efficacy and high risk of severe wound infection, sepsis, and gastrointestinal hemorrhage.
> 
> **3. Surgical Decompression & Stabilization**
> - **Timing:** Emergent surgical decompression within **$< 24\text{ hours}$** (and ideally within $< 8–12\text{ hours}$) for incomplete spinal cord injury or progressive deterioration significantly improves neurological outcomes (STASCIS trial).
> - **Techniques:** Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), posterior cervical instrumented fusion, or posterior thoracolumbar laminectomy and pedicle screw stabilization.
> - **Closed Cervical Traction (Gardner-Wells Tongs):** Indicated for awake, cooperative patients with facet dislocation/subluxation under serial lateral X-ray and neurological monitoring.

> [!warning]- Contraindications & Pitfalls
> - **Do NOT use Succinylcholine after 48 hours post-SCI:** Causes lethal hyperkalemia due to massive up-regulation of acetylcholine receptors along denervated skeletal muscle.
> - **Never administer high-dose steroids routinely:** Complications (pneumonia, GI bleeding, death) outweigh marginal motor score gains.
> - **Do not clear C-spine based on plain X-rays alone:** CT is the mandatory standard of care in high-risk trauma.

> [!error]- Complications
> - Autonomic Dysreflexia (late complication in injuries above T6: massive paroxysmal hypertension, bradycardia, headache, and flushing triggered by noxious stimuli like full bladder or fecal impaction).
> - Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (extremely high risk; initiate LMWH thromboprophylaxis within 24–72h post-injury).
> - Neurogenic bladder (recurrent UTIs) and neurogenic bowel.
> - Decubitus pressure ulcers.

> [!success]- Typical Scenario
> A 32-year-old construction worker falls 15 feet from scaffolding, landing on his upper back. He is conscious but immediately complains of severe mid-thoracic back pain and cannot feel or move his legs. EMS places a rigid cervical collar and transports him on a vacuum splint. In the trauma bay, BP is 82/46 mmHg with a heart rate of 52 bpm; skin is warm and dry. Sensory exam demonstrates a complete sensory level at the umbilicus (T10), with 0/5 power in all lower extremity muscle groups and absent rectal tone. Neurogenic shock is recognized; 1L crystalloid bolus and a Norepinephrine infusion are started, achieving a MAP of 88 mmHg. Spine CT reveals a T9–T10 flexion-distraction burst fracture with 70% spinal canal retropulsion. Emergent MRI confirms severe cord compression and cord edema without transection. He is transferred urgently to the operating room at hour 4 post-injury for T8–T11 posterior instrumented reduction, laminectomy, and pedicle screw fusion. Postoperatively, MAP is maintained > 85 mmHg in the neuro-ICU; by week 3, he demonstrates returning sensory sparing in S4–S5 and partial hip flexor motor recovery.

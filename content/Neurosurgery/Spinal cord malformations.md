> [!abstract] Spinal cord malformations
> Congenital anomalies of the embryonic neural tube and spinal canal, broadly classified into **Open Spinal Dysraphism** (e.g., Myelomeningocele, exposed neural tissue without skin covering) and **Closed Spinal Dysraphism** (e.g., Lipomyelomeningocele, Split Cord Malformations / Diastematomyelia, Thickened Filum Terminale). Their shared pathological hallmark is **Tethered Cord Syndrome (TCS)**, where abnormal mechanical fixation of the conus medullaris prevents normal physiological movement, producing progressive ischemic and mechanical traction myelopathy during childhood growth or adult flexion activities.

> [!note] Pathophysiology
> - **Embryological Mechanisms:**
>   - **Primary Neurulation Defect (Weeks 3–4 of gestation):** Failure of the embryonic neural plate to fold and close into a tubular structure $\rightarrow$ **Myelomeningocele** (herniation of spinal cord and meninges through open posterior vertebral arches, CSF leakage, invariably associated with Chiari II malformation).
>   - **Secondary Neurulation Defect (Weeks 5–6):** Failure of the caudal cell mass to regress $\rightarrow$ low-lying conus, caudal lipomas, and **thickened tight filum terminale** ($> 2\text{ mm}$ thickness, fatty infiltration).
>   - **Gastrulation Anomalies:** Persistent neurenteric canal causes **Split Cord Malformation (Diastematomyelia)**: sagittal division of the spinal cord into two hemicords by an osseous or fibrous midline spur.
> - **Tethered Cord Syndrome (TCS) Biomechanics:**
>   - In normal development, the vertebral column outgrows the spinal cord, so the conus medullaris ascends from the sacrum to the adult **L1–L2 level** by 3 months of life.
>   - When the cord is tethered to the sacrum/vertebrae (by a tight filum, lipoma, or bony spur), longitudinal traction places chronic tension on the conus.
>   - Tension causes microcirculatory stretching, impaired oxidative metabolism (mitochondrial hypoxia), and progressive sensorimotor, urological, and orthopedic deficits exacerbated by growth spurts or physical exertion.

> [!tip]- Clinical PEARLS
> - **Cutaneous Stigmata of Spina Bifida Occulta (The "Signposts of Dysraphism"):**
>   - Look at the midline lumbosacral skin in newborns and infants: **Tuft of hair (hypertrichosis / faun tail)**, dermal sinus pit (above the gluteal cleft), subcutaneous lipoma, atypical sacral dimple ($> 5\text{ mm}$ depth or $> 2.5\text{ cm}$ above anus), hemangioma, or tail-like appendage.
>   - Presence warrants formal spine MRI to diagnose occult closed dysraphism *before* irreversible deficits develop.
> - **The Conus Level on Sagittal MRI:** A conus medullaris terminating **below the lower border of L2** is by definition abnormal and establishes low conus / spinal cord tethering.
> - **Progressive Foot Deformity & Scoliosis:** High-arched foot (**pes cavus**), progressive claw toes, asymmetric leg/foot growth, or rapid progression of thoracic-lumbar scoliosis in a growing child is classic for tethered cord syndrome.
> - **Folic Acid Prevention:** Maternal peri-conceptional folic acid supplementation ($400\ \mu\text{g/day}$ in standard risk; $4–5\text{ mg/day}$ in high risk with previous affected pregnancy) reduces primary neural tube defects by up to $70\%$.
> - **MOMS Trial (NEJM 2011):** Fetal intrauterine repair of myelomeningocele ($< 26\text{ weeks}$ gestation) significantly reduces the need for hydrocephalus shunting and improves motor outcome at 30 months compared to standard postnatal repair.

> [!attention]- Clinical Presentation
> **Pediatric Presentation (Onset during Growth Spurts)**
> - **Cutaneous Markers:** Midline lumbosacral skin lesions (hairy patch, dimple, lipoma, capillary hemangioma) in $> 80\%$ of closed dysraphisms.
> - **Orthopedic Manifestations:** Progressive equinovarus or pes cavus foot deformities, asymmetric leg length/calf atrophy, progressive neuromuscular scoliosis or lordosis.
> - **Urological Manifestations (Most sensitive functional marker):** Delayed potty training, new-onset daytime enuresis in a previously continent child, recurrent urinary tract infections from high-pressure neurogenic bladder.
> - **Motor/Gait Difficulties:** Frequent tripping, clumsy running, stiffness in the legs.
> 
> **Adult Presentation**
> - Chronic low back and leg pain exacerbated by bending forward or heavy lifting.
> - Progressive bilateral leg weakness, numbness in dermatomes L5–S1.
> - Urge incontinence, incomplete bladder emptying, erectile dysfunction.

> [!check]- Diagnostic Criteria
> - **Radiological Criteria for Tethered Cord:**
>   - Conus medullaris terminating below L2 on sagittal T1/T2 MRI.
>   - Thickened filum terminale ($> 2\text{ mm}$ on axial view at L5–S1) $\pm$ fatty infiltration (hyperintense on T1).
>   - Posterior displacement of the spinal cord against the dorsal dural sac.
> - **Clinical TCS Criteria:** Documented low-lying or mechanically tethered cord + $\ge 1$ objective progressive neurological, urological (abnormal urodynamics), or orthopedic deficit.

> [!fail]- Further Investigations
> - **Whole-Spine MRI with Sagittal and Axial T1, T2, and Fat-Suppression (STIR) Sequences (Gold Standard):** Identifies conus position, filum thickness, intradural lipoma, bony spur in diastematomyelia, and associated Chiari malformations or syrinx.
> - **Spinal Ultrasound (Newborns $< 3–6\text{ months}$):** Excellent non-invasive screening tool through unossified posterior cartilaginous arches; visualizes conus level and real-time pulsatile nerve root motion.
> - **Urodynamic Studies (UDS):** Essential baseline and surveillance investigation: identifies detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia (DSD), elevated detrusor leak point pressure ($> 40\text{ cmH}_2\text{O}$ risks upper renal tract deterioration), and reduced bladder compliance.
> - **CT Spine with 3D Reconstructions:** Crucial in split cord malformations (diastematomyelia) to identify the exact osseous morphology of the dividing spur prior to surgical excision.

> [!bug]- Red Flags
> - Clear CSF leakage from an open sacral defect in a newborn (open myelomeningocele: emergent infection and meningitis risk).
> - New-onset urinary incontinence or acute urinary retention.
> - Rapidly progressive leg weakness or asymmetric foot drop.
> - Cutaneous dermal sinus tract with recurrent bouts of bacterial meningitis or spinal epidural abscess.

> [!todo]- Differentials
> - Idiopathic Juvenile Scoliosis (normal conus level on MRI, no cutaneous stigmata).
> - Cerebral Palsy or hereditary spastic paraplegia (spastic gait without focal spinal cord tethering on MRI).
> - Charcot-Marie-Tooth Disease (hereditary motor sensory neuropathy causing bilateral pes cavus, but with peripheral nerve conduction slowing).
> - Sacrococcygeal Teratoma (neoplasm of the caudal region without primary intraspinal neural extension).

> [!summary]- Management
> **1. Emergent Newborn Management (Open Myelomeningocele)**
> - Immediate neonatal resuscitation under sterile conditions; keep newborn prone or lateral.
> - Cover open neural placode with sterile warm saline-soaked non-adherent dressings and sterile plastic wrap (prevents heat loss and desiccation).
> - Initiate IV broad-spectrum antibiotics (Ampicillin + Gentamicin).
> - **Urgent Surgical Closure within 24–48 hours:** Multi-layered closure of placode, dura, lumbodorsal fascia, and skin to prevent ventriculitis/meningitis and preserve neurological function.
> - **In Utero Fetal Surgery:** Repair between 19 and 25.9 weeks of gestation in eligible mothers under the MOMS trial protocol.
> 
> **2. Neurosurgical Untethering (Tethered Cord Release)**
> - **Elective Untethering Surgery:** Indicated for symptomatic tethered cord, progressive functional decline, or asymptomatic low-lying conus with tight filum or lipoma before irreversible damage occurs.
> - **Surgical Techniques:**
>   - *Section of Filum Terminale (Filumectomy):* L5 or S1 mini-laminotomy, identification of the filum (distinguished from roots by lack of motor response on direct electrical stimulation and characteristic vessel pattern), bipolar coagulation and transection, allowing the conus to retract cephalad.
>   - *Resection of Diastematomyelia Spur:* Laminectomy, extradural/intradural excision of the dividing osseous or cartilaginous septum, and reconstruction of a single unified dural tube.
>   - *Lipomyelomeningocele Untethering:* Careful microsurgical debonding of the subcutaneous lipoma from the dorsal neural placode under continuous intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM: motor evoked potentials, EMG, and bulbocavernosus reflex).
> 
> **3. Multidisciplinary Longitudinal Care**
> - Pediatric urology (clean intermittent catheterization, anticholinergics to protect kidneys), pediatric orthopedics (foot correction, bracing for scoliosis), and physical therapy.

> [!warning]- Contraindications & Pitfalls
> - **NEVER probe, puncture, or inject a midline sacral dermal sinus pit:** Can introduce bacteria directly into the subarachnoid space causing fatal bacterial meningitis.
> - **Do not coagulate nerve roots during untethering:** Strict continuous neuromonitoring with bipolar stimulation is mandatory to avoid permanent sphincter denervation.

> [!error]- Complications
> - Retethering (10–20%; postoperative scarring and dural adhesions can tether the conus again years later, requiring redo untethering).
> - Postoperative CSF leakage / fistula through thin skin flaps.
> - Worsening neurogenic bladder or fecal incontinence.
> - Hydrocephalus (frequently triggered post-closure of open myelomeningocele, requiring VP shunting or ETV).

> [!success]- Typical Scenario
> A 6-year-old boy is brought to the pediatric neurosurgery clinic because of progressive clumsiness and frequent falls over the past 8 months. His mother notes that his left foot has developed an increasingly high arch (pes cavus) and that he recently began having episodes of daytime urinary wetting despite having been completely toilet-trained at age 3. Examination reveals a small tuft of fine brown hair over the upper sacrum (L5–S1) and mild weakness of left ankle dorsiflexion (4/5) with an absent left Achilles reflex. Spine MRI reveals a low-lying conus medullaris terminating at the L4 vertebral level, held by a thick, fatty filum terminale measuring 3.5 mm in diameter, with a small 2 cm associated conus syrinx. Urodynamic studies demonstrate detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia with elevated bladder pressures. He undergoes an elective micro-laminotomy at L5–S1 with intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring; the fatty filum terminale is identified, stimulated without motor response, and transected. The conus is observed to immediately retract cephalad by 8 mm. Over the following 6 months, his urinary control completely normalizes, his gait stabilizes, and repeat MRI confirms complete resolution of the syrinx.
